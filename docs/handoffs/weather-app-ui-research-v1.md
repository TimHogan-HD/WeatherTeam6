# Users want weather apps that admit doubt

Users mostly want a weather app to answer one question at a glance: what is coming, when, and how sure is the app? The feature people remember from Dark Sky is a sentence they could check against the sky ("light rain starting in 4 minutes and ending in 17"), shown on a simple timeline. Big data dumps and bare icons are not what they recall. Surveys and controlled experiments back this up. People check forecasts about 115 to 118 times a month, mostly just "to know what the weather will be like". Temperature and precipitation are what they look at. Uncertainty information shown as numbers improves decisions and raises trust, while symbols alone and low/medium/high categories do worse. Complaints follow the same pattern from the other side: in one aggregation of negative reviews, accuracy, ads and paywalls make up about 70% of complaints, and redesigns that remove features cause outsized anger among enthusiasts. Climbers are heavy, sceptical users. They cross-check several sources, want the forecast for the crag rather than the nearest town, and have watched at least one drying model get switched off after it called wet sandstone dry. For WeatherTeam6, the evidence points toward a short verdict sentence with timing, a score that shows its inputs and how sure it is, honest spread on the 7-day and trip views, and visible data freshness. The weakest evidence covers the app's core display: no study tests how people read a composite 0-100 activity score, so that design remains a judgment call for the owner.

## People check often, want the coming hours, and remember a checkable sentence

Forecast use is close to universal and has stayed steady while the channel changed. A 2006 US survey found adults accessed forecasts about **115 times a month**, and the 2022 replication found **117.8**, a statistically flat change. Over the same period, "active" sources such as phones and the NWS website grew while TV and radio fell ([CoFU2, AMS 2024](https://ametsoc.org/sites/ams/assets/File/policy/CoFU2_JKL_2024.pdf)). In a 2021 survey, **74.3% used a phone weather app for routine forecasts against 6.1% for television** ([Krocak et al. 2024](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/67719)). A 2023 YouGov poll put the app share at 53% under different wording. It found that people look at **temperature (75%) and precipitation (60%)** far more than humidity or wind (37% each), feels-like (35%) or air quality (22%), and that 36% always or usually look at radar ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/45672-how-and-where-americans-get-information-weather)). The most frequent use in both CoFU2 waves was simply "to know what the weather will be like". The strongest predictor of how often someone checks was the share of their job spent outside. People who spend more leisure time outdoors were *less* satisfied with what they get ([CoFU2](https://ametsoc.org/sites/ams/assets/File/policy/CoFU2_JKL_2024.pdf)). That is a documented gap that outdoor-specific products are trying to fill.

What users praise matches that monitoring habit. The Hacker News thread "Eulogy for Dark Sky, a data visualization masterpiece" reached 467 points. In it, users remember being able to "simply glance at it", and recall statements like "Light rain starting in 4 minutes and ending in 17 minutes" coming true ([HN mirror](https://hn.nuxt.dev/item/46565132)). Hello Weather is praised specifically for "rain timing" ([Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/products/hello-weather/reviews)). Apple absorbed Dark Sky's data, but a TechRadar writer found its hourly rain buried in a sub-menu and its graph "tougher to read" ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/opinion/apples-bad-weather-app-is-making-me-long-for-dark-sky)). Apple Community users ask where the next-hour rain feature even is ([Apple Discussions](https://discussions-cn-prz.apple.com/thread/254517646)). The lesson is that **a capability users cannot find or read counts as missing**. Design commentary has said the same for over a decade. ustwo argued that apps open on *current* conditions when people want what is *coming*, and that both clutter and over-minimalism fail ([ustwo](https://ustwo.com/blog/weather-apps-ignoring-form-and-function/)). A 2022 BBC News app panel preferred an hourly forecast for their current location, with a timestamp ([portfolio write-up of BBC research](https://chenxiuwww.cargo.site/discovery-research-insights)).

The layout has settled into a common grammar. Apple, Acme, Mercury and Pixel Weather all use one vertical scroll: current conditions, an hourly strip or graph, a 10-day list with high/low range bars, then tappable modules ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/guide/ios-16-weather/); [MacStories on Acme](https://www.macstories.net/?p=196191); [MacStories on Mercury](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/mercury-weather-a-crystal-clear-design-for-every-apple-device/)). Two newer moves point the same way. Acme keeps one hourly graph and lets a row of chips change its metric, and its minute-by-minute rain graph appears only when rain is coming ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/?p=196191)). Apple's iOS 27 beta adds a "Highlights" line flagging notable weather in roughly the next day, plus Precipitation and Wind views that refocus the hourly and daily summaries on that one variable ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/06/17/apple-weather-gets-two-brand-new-features-in-ios-27/)). Whether those shipped in the final release was not verified. Users disagree about density. One HN commenter credits Dark Sky's focus and finds newer apps "so much more busy and dense". Another wants "a toggle for 'denser graphs'" ([HN mirror](https://hn.nuxt.dev/item/46565132)). The common ground in the notes is glanceable first, with depth one tap down. That is an inference from these sources, not a measured preference.

## Numbers with uncertainty beat icons and categories, even though percentages confuse

Probability of precipitation is widely misread, yet it still helps. Rates of reading "30% chance of rain" correctly range from about **19% of the US public to about 65% in New York**, and only 55% of weather forecasters themselves picked the official meaning ([Stephens et al. 2019](https://gc.copernicus.org/articles/2/101/2019/); [Gigerenzer et al. 2005](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/riskan/v25y2005i3p623-629.html)). The University of Washington found that **stating the chance of *no* rain alongside it improved understanding** ([UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2009/04/14/peoples-misperceptions-cloud-their-understanding-of-rainy-weather-forecasts)). At low percentages, misreading shifts toward intensity: with a 30% forecast, 22% to 27% of respondents read it as being about duration, intensity or totals ([Vaughn et al. 2024](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/61161)). The largest experiment, the Met Office Weather Game with **8,220 players**, found that uncertainty information "greatly improved decision-making ability and did not cause confusion". Formats that included the percentage beat "bar plus low/medium/high rating" and "bar only" formats carrying the same information. Symbols alone produced the worst rain decisions, and a line chart beat a table for temperature ([Stephens et al. 2019 PDF](https://gc.copernicus.org/articles/2/101/2019/gc-2-101-2019.pdf)). About 70% of the public prefer or are willing to receive uncertainty information, only 7% prefer a deterministic forecast, and most people put their own uncertainty around a single number anyway ([Stephens et al. 2019, citing Morss et al. 2008](https://gc.copernicus.org/articles/2/101/2019/)).

Users want amount and timing more than probability. HN commenters find percentages less useful than mm/hr in rainy places ([HN mirror](https://hn.nuxt.dev/item/46565132)). The angriest accuracy complaints involve signals that contradict each other or sound overconfident: "30% chance of rain" shown beside a 0 mm forecast ([Apple Discussions](https://discussions-kr-prz.apple.com/thread/254877591)), or "100% chance of rain, didn't see a drop" ([unstar.app](https://unstar.app/blog/weather-apps-ranked-by-user-complaints-2026)). Climbers made the same point in 2015 about Mountain Project's rain icon, which "just shows rain whether it's a 10% chance or rain or a 90% chance". They asked for the percentage instead ([Mountain Project](https://www.mountainproject.com/forum/topic/110693173/pessimistic-weather-feature)).

Trust research supports showing doubt. Inaccuracy hurts trust more than forecasts that change between updates ([Su, Burgeno and Joslyn 2021](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/51143)). **Adding probabilistic information softened the loss of trust after a wrong forecast** and produced better decisions ([Joslyn and LeClerc 2012](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/users/aldous/157/Papers/leclerk.pdf); [Burgeno and Joslyn 2020](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/2606e30e-b9a8-4669-9a3f-57914fff773c/full)). In the Joslyn lab work, participants judged acknowledged uncertainty as "more honest". People already lower their confidence with lead time, from **4.07 out of 5 at day 1 to 2.54 at day 10**. The same authors criticise apps for "excessively, and potentially unrealistically, detailed forecasts" far ahead with no confidence caveat ([Vaughn et al. 2024](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/61161)). The market has taken this up. Acme Weather, launched in February 2026 by the Dark Sky founders, draws alternate model runs as grey lines behind its own forecast so the width of the spread shows reliability. Its founder calls apps that "just get their best guess" his biggest pet peeve ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/23/ex-apple-team-launches-acme-weather-a-new-take-on-weather-forecasting/); [MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/?p=196191)). Windy shows Meteoblue predictability as coloured dots next to each day ([Windy](https://www.windy.com/articles/43904)), and Google's WeatherNext 2 generates hundreds of possible outcomes per run ([Google](https://blog.google/technology/google-deepmind/weathernext-2/)). Format choices still carry risk. All seven line and fan-chart temperature displays tested by Tak et al. produced an optimistic bias ([cited in Stephens et al. 2019](https://gc.copernicus.org/articles/2/101/2019/)). The hurricane cone, a hard-edged shape, is read as the storm growing ([Ruginski/Padilla et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5626802)). No single best format exists.

## Accuracy, ads and paywalls make up 70% of complaints, and removed features cause the loudest backlash

One aggregation of 1 to 3 star reviews across eight major apps attributes **28% of complaints to accuracy, 23% to ads, 19% to subscriptions or paywalls, 12% to notification spam and 9% to redesigns**. Its methodology is unpublished, so treat it as indicative ([unstar.app](https://unstar.app/blog/weather-apps-ranked-by-user-complaints-2026)). Sample lines include "Had to scroll past 4 ads to see tomorrow", "Notified me about rain in a city I don't live in", "Widget shows wrong city" and "Where's the radar button?". Ads that cover the forecast drew 37.4% of The Weather Channel's negative reviews in a separate sample ([unstar.app](https://unstar.app/blog/weather-channel-app-ads-before-alerts-radar-reviews-2026)). The Met Office accessibility audit flagged unpausable ad animations as a defect ([GDS](https://accessibility.blog.gov.uk/2025/01/14/accessibility-audit-of-the-met-office-app-outcomes-and-learnings/)). Business models shape the screen. Mercury puts extra saved locations and widgets behind a subscription, and Clime gates alerts ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/reviews/mercury-weather-a-crystal-clear-design-for-every-apple-device/)). Carrot's move to subscriptions pushed some users to one-time-purchase apps ([MacRumors](https://forums.macrumors.com/threads/carrot-weather-5-0-launches-with-fresh-design-more-snarky-dialogue-and-customization-options.2282291/page-5)).

Redesigns that drop features hurt enthusiasts most. The refreshed Met Office app (January 2026) drew complaints about missing saved locations and pressure data, and some old features came back ([Met Office](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2026/how-the-refreshed-met-office-app-is-taking-shape)). MeteoSwiss 3.0 was criticised for capping saved locations at five ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/meteoswiss/id589772015)), and Weather Underground's v6 redesign shed features and ratings ([ccstartup](https://ccstartup.com/blog/?p=12762)). Power users ask to compare models and pick sources. Mountain Project climbers use Windy and Spotwx for this, and one wants a single reliable app "instead of many browser tabs" ([Mountain Project](https://www.mountainproject.com/forum/message/201260685)). Flowx shows one graph per data source on double tap ([Flowx](https://flowx.io/help/graphs/)). Privacy rarely appears in reviews, but it has legal history: Los Angeles sued The Weather Channel app over location data in 2019, and the 2020 settlement required a clearer opt-in prompt and disclosures ([Proskauer](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2020/08/18/eclipsed-by-evolving-law-policy-and-technology-seminal-mobile-location-data-case-settled/)). Germany's DWD dropped Google Analytics from its warning app, citing user privacy ([iphone-ticker](https://www.iphone-ticker.de/dwd-app-warnwetter-in-version-5-0-veroeffentlicht-232969/)).

## Climbers cross-check, and they distrust models that claim to see inside the rock

Climbing-conditions products are now numerous, and most reduce conditions to a short banded scale. ClimbingWeather uses Prime, Good, Fair, Iffy and Skip ([ClimbingWeather](https://climbingweather.com/area/560/detail/USA/Nevada/Red-Rocks)). Climbit gives 0 to 5 stars ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/ca/app/climbit-score/id6517296523)). CragReport scores 0 to 100 but displays it in four coloured bands (90+ excellent, 80-89 good, 60-79 fair, below 60 poor). It also lets users override the rock type, sends email alerts when thresholds are met, and accepts condition reports ([CragReport FAQ](https://www.cragreport.com/faq)). Climbing Forecast UK rates each day Good, Caution or Poor. Each Good day carries "check rock is fully dry before climbing". The page shows per-factor rows (temperature, rain, wind, gusts, humidity) with short cause notes such as breezy weather helping drying, and a "Rain 48h" and "Last rain" block. Its wet-sandstone wording says not to climb, and the day after rain is rated Caution because "sandstone may still hold subsurface moisture" ([Climbing Forecast UK](https://climbingforecast.co.uk/crag/stone-farm-rocks)). One Boulder climber describes the role such a score plays: "my first check is climbit score now, then I dial it in with another weather app" ([Mountain Project](https://www.mountainproject.com/forum/message/200672350)).

Climbers are sceptical of these scores. Their talk is about cross-checking: NWS hourly graphs, Windy, Meteoblue, Spotwx and Mountain-Forecast's elevation bands. Users note that one NWS grid cell over can mean thousands of feet of elevation ([Mountain Project](https://www.mountainproject.com/forum/topic/202239259/best-weather-forecasting-serviceapp)). They value summit or crag point forecasts over "the nearest town" ([Mountain Project](https://www.mountainproject.com/forum/message/201260685)). Two apps disagreeing by 14°F for the same crag set off a thread of hand-comparing grid cells ([Mountain Project](https://www.mountainproject.com/forum/topic/124560541/climbingweathercom-vs-mountain-project-which-is-more-accurate)). A UKC thread, known only from a snippet, describes a sandstone drying website that was "totally wrong under certain conditions (basically half of the year)" and was turned off ([UKC](https://www.ukclimbing.com/forums/rock_talk/wet_rock_monitoring_app-780635)). Drying guidance itself varies: 24 to 72 hours for desert sandstone ([Mountain Hardwear](https://www.mountainhardwear.com/learn/know-how/rock-climbing/rock-n-rain.html)), and "5 days to even a week" after big storms ([Touchstone](https://touchstoneclimbing.com/can-i-climb-on-wet-rock-a-guide-to-california-climbing-after-the-rain/)). A UKC thread also reports a crag app showing stale data ([UKC](https://www.ukclimbing.com/forums/rockfax/weather_forecast-726577)).

Adjacent sports show how to rate conditions without overclaiming. The avalanche danger scale pairs each of five levels with a number, a colour and a plain travel action ([avalanche.org](https://avalanche.org/avalanche-encyclopedia/danger-scale/)). Surfline's April 2025 scale lets only forecasters who have observed conditions award "Good" or "Epic" ([Surfline](https://www.surfline.com/lp/whatsnew/releases/apr-25)). OpenSnow calls its Powder Quality rating "a guide, not an absolute" and explains why it can diverge from snow depth ([OpenSnow](https://support.opensnow.com/feature-guides/powder-quality)). The evidence gap is the composite score itself. No study was found on how people read activity scores, 0-100 against 1-10 scales, or whether showing components builds trust. With established indices, awareness far exceeds action: 64% had heard of the UV index and over 90% of them described it correctly, but only 38% changed behaviour ([CDC MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00047416.htm)). No climbing product found says it validates its score against outcomes. Long-range trip planning with confidence that improves over time appears in no climbing product found. Windy's predictability dots are the nearest UI precedent ([Windy](https://www.windy.com/articles/43904)).

## Options for WeatherTeam6, with evidence and confidence

These are options for the owner to decide, not decisions. **Confidence** rates how well the evidence supports the option for this app. **Basis** separates what the sources show (fact) from what this report infers for a few climbers using a single crag score (inference). Several options touch rock, safety or design, which are the owner's call under the project's rules.

| Option | Basis | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Lead each crag with a checkable sentence that carries timing**, such as "Dry until 15:00, showers 15:00 to 17:00, about 3 mm", above or beside the Crag A number. | Fact: Dark Sky's timing sentence is the most-praised feature, "rain timing" is praised in Hello Weather, and "when precipitation would occur" ranked top in 2006. Inference: a crag verdict sentence carries this over. | High |
| **2. Keep the 0-100 number but pair it with a word band, a colour and an action line**, including a middle "caution" state, instead of replacing the number with words. | Fact: numbers beat low/medium/high categories at equal information (Stephens 2019), and climbing and avalanche scales band into named levels with actions. Inference: number plus band gets both benefits. Untested for composite scores. | Medium |
| **3. Show the score's inputs under it**: rain in the last 48 h, time of last rain, humidity, wind, temperature, sun on the wall, each with a short cause note. | Fact: Climbing Forecast UK and CragReport do this, and climbers cross-check inputs. Inference: visible inputs make the score a "first check" users can verify rather than a verdict they second-guess. | Medium-High |
| **4. Present Crag A as unvalidated**, for example as "a guide", or by holding back the top band unless a recent Report backs it up (Surfline's observer rule). | Fact: Surfline and OpenSnow both cap confidence, and a drying model was switched off after being confidently wrong. Inference: an unvalidated score earns trust by claiming less. Product and safety decision. | Medium (direction), Low (specific mechanism) |
| **5. Show spread on the 7-day and trip views**, as range bands, faint alternate lines or per-day confidence marks, and let a trip show its confidence narrowing as the date approaches. | Fact: uncertainty improves decisions and buffers trust loss, users' confidence falls with lead time, and Acme and Windy ship this. Inference: narrowing confidence suits trip planning. Format risk: fan charts bias optimistic, and hard edges get read as physical boundaries. | High (showing spread), Medium (format) |
| **6. Show rain as amount and timing first**, a percentage only if numeric with its meaning stated, and never a percentage that contradicts the amount beside it. | Fact: PoP is widely misread, stating the "no rain" side helps, and contradictory signals draw the angriest reviews. | High |
| **7. Put data freshness and source on screen** ("updated 10:40, from <model>") wherever a reading appears. | Fact: Pixel added a forecast timestamp in 2026, the BBC panel wanted one, and climbers complained about stale data. Inference: this fits the project's attribution rule. | Medium-High |
| **8. Keep two layers**: the verdict and one timeline at a glance, with radar, model detail and raw numbers one tap down. Consider one chart slot with a variable switch (score, rain, temperature, wind) instead of several charts. | Fact: Acme's chip row and Apple's iOS 27 views converge on this, and the density split is real. Inference: one slot keeps the crag screen short. | Medium |
| **9. Add a "which of my crags is best on day X" comparison**, in a list or on the Map tab's pins. | Fact: users missed ClimbingWeather's crag map, and a UKC poster picks the dry crag on arrival. Inference: comparison is the decision climbers actually make. | Medium |
| **10. Show recent Reports from partners next to the forecast.** | Fact: Acme's founder calls nearby reports the most reliable signal, and CragReport collects condition reports. Inference: the Report button already gathers this data, so surfacing it costs little. | Medium |
| **11. Threshold alerts ("crag X becomes climbable")** stay parked unless the owner reopens them. | Fact: CragReport and Acme offer them, and notification spam is 12% of complaints. The project's rules park a notification channel. | Low-Medium (owner decision) |
| **12. Keep saved crags, views and controls stable across redesigns**, and ask before removing anything. | Fact: backlash at the Met Office, MeteoSwiss and Weather Underground, plus "Where's the radar button?". | Medium |
| **13. Keep the privacy and accessibility basics**: ask for location only when needed and say why, add no third-party ad or analytics SDKs, use native controls, and respect reduced motion. | Fact: the LA settlement, DWD dropping analytics, and the GDS audit. Inference: low cost for a small PWA. | Medium |

Three tensions remain for the owner. First, words against numbers: activity apps band their scores into words, while the strongest experiment found numbers outperform categories. Showing both is a hedge that has not been tested. Second, how much safety wording to show: Climbing Forecast UK attaches "check rock is fully dry" to every good day, and repeated caveats can become noise. That is a rock and safety call. Third, density: the evidence does not settle how much to show by default, and the owner and partners are better judges of their own use than the general-population surveys.

## Conclusion

Taken together, the sources show that the weather apps users trust are not the ones that sound most certain. They are the ones whose claims can be checked: a time the rain starts, the inputs behind a rating, how widely the models disagree, and when the data was fetched. That matters more for WeatherTeam6 than for a general app, because its central number is a model of something no one can see, moisture inside the rock. The one documented failure of that kind of model ended with the site switched off. Each option above that makes Crag A easier to check makes it easier to correct when it is wrong.

The research also points to a gap that WeatherTeam6 is unusually well placed to fill. No climbing product found validates its score against outcomes, and none shows trip confidence narrowing over weeks. Outdoor users are the least satisfied group in the national survey. The Report button's forecast checks, combined with lead-time spread on trip outlooks, would turn the app's main weakness, an unvalidated score, into something it measures and shows. No competitor found offers this, and the trust literature suggests users reward it.
