Overview and Hourly, from the weather app research

Two screens, each with the current layout and two variants built from the research report (docs/handoffs/weather-app-ui-research-v1.md). Every variant keeps the copy rules: no word from the score, a reading is a label and a value, weather beside every score. The figures are an invented Thursday at Taylors Falls, where the day scores 85 on its dry morning while rain arrives at 2 PM. Drag across any Hourly chart to read an hour.

Owner picks, 2026-11-24: Overview A + B; Hourly A + B with rain drawn as C (chance row on top, amount bars below); none of section 3 for now. Each switch opens on the pick; the other variants stay for comparison. Still open: "Each forecast" or "Range" for the rain spread, and whether partners' forecast checks show on Overview.

B · Spread shown

  • Temperature with its band: the p10–p90 range of the ensemble behind the median line, keyed in words ("8 in 10 runs fall inside").
  • Rain with each model's line behind the median columns. GEM keeps Thursday dry; three models put the shower between 1 and 7 PM. A reader sees why the count is 3 of 4.
  • Freshness under the name.
  • Rain shows chance and amount together: bars are the amount in inches on the left scale; the white line is the chance of measurable rain on a 0–100% right scale, the share of ensemble members wet that hour (precip_chance_pct, already on /hourly). The read hour's chip gives both in words, chance first ("3 PM · 70% chance, 0.06 in"). Two scales on one chart is the cost; the key names which is which. precip_chance_pct is null when no ensemble member reached the hour: the line breaks there and the chip leaves the chance out, never drawn or written as 0%.
  • Rain spread, two ways, behind a small toggle: "Each forecast" draws every model as a dotted line in its own colour (violet, yellow, pink; GEM, dry all day, in grey), with the names in a key under the chart rather than on it; "Range" shades from the driest forecast to the wettest, the same reading as the temperature band. Pick one; the build ships one.
  • Readable at a glance: the temperature band is labelled "likely range", the read hour's chip gives the range in figures ("41°F, likely 38–45°"; "0.06 in, forecasts 0–.08"), and one plain line under each chart says how to read it.

Research: Acme's founding idea is alternate forecasts as faint lines; Windy shows predictability per day. Fan charts biased readers optimistic in one study, so the band is soft-edged and the median stays the brightest mark (report option 5).

Data (B): the temperature band exists (temp_c_p10, temp_c_p90; --rangeFrost is already a token). Per-model hourly rain is not on the response: the server reads it for the median and the split, then drops it. It would be a new field on /hourly.

Current · two scales

  • Bars are inches on the left scale; the white line is the chance on a 0–100% right scale.
  • The cost: where the line crosses a bar means nothing, yet the eye reads it as a relation. Two scales on one chart are the pattern most often misread (Few; Isenberg et al. 2011), and the right edge eats 24 px of a 390 px screen.

C · Chance row on top · picked 2026-11-24

  • A row of hour cells above the bars, one per hour, darker the likelier, with the percentage written in each cell from 10% up. Bars below are the amount on the inch scale, as today.
  • Each measure keeps the mark that suits it: a percentage is a share, so a shade and a figure; an amount is a quantity, so a bar on a scale.
  • Same shape as A's day strip, so the hourly screen has one idiom for "per hour" rather than two.
  • Reads the early hours too: 30% at 1 PM shows though no rain is expected yet in the median.

Recommended. No second scale, no crossing marks, and both figures are readable without scrubbing.

D · Chance bars, amount written

  • Bar height is the chance on one 0–100 scale; the amount is written above each wet bar (".06").
  • The pattern Apple Weather and most phone apps use, so it is familiar.
  • The cost: the amount, which is what wets the rock, becomes small text. A 90% chance of a trace and a 90% chance of half an inch look the same until the reader reads the figures.

F · D swapped: amount bars, chance written

  • Bar height is the amount on the inch scale; the chance is written above each hour in the chance colour (violet), the way D writes the amount in the bar colour.
  • The amount, which decides how wet the rock gets, keeps the scale; the chance is one glance up, no second axis, no fade to decode.
  • Hours with a chance but no median amount (1 PM, 30%) show the figure on the baseline, so the build-up before the shower still reads.
  • The cost: the chance is figures only, so its shape across the day is read number by number rather than seen. Chances under 10% are left unwritten to keep the dry hours quiet; a null chance is written "—", so a gap never reads as a low chance. The bar still draws if the amount is known.

E · Amount bars, faded by chance

  • Bar height is the amount; a bar is fainter the less likely it is, and the chance is written above it.
  • The amount keeps the scale, which suits a crag screen: wetness is what decides drying.
  • The cost: opacity is the least precise visual channel (Cleveland and McGill 1984); the fade is a hint, so the figures carry the reading. Hours with a chance but no median amount show only a figure on the baseline.

Data, all variants: amount is the hourly median, chance is precip_chance_pct (the share of ensemble members wet), both already on /hourly. A null chance is never 0%: in C it is no cell at all (a 0% hour is a faint drawn cell), in D no bar, and in E and F a "—" where the figure would be. E and F leave chances under 10% unwritten.

The chosen look carries into B's spread views (each forecast's line or the range sit on the amount scale, so C and E keep them; D would need them redrawn as chance).

Word band

  • A word beside the number, in four rungs with fixed colours, and one action line per rung. Every climbing app found does this: ClimbingWeather (Prime to Skip), CragReport (four colour bands), Climbing Forecast UK (Good, Caution, Poor), the avalanche danger scale.
  • The action lines would be: Good "Rock should be dry and cool enough for friction"; Fair "Climbable, conditions not ideal"; Caution "Check the rock is dry before you climb"; Poor "Wet or too warm, likely not worth the drive".

Breaks copy rule 1 (no word mapped from the score; no opinion on whether to climb). The rule exists because a word graded an unvalidated estimate: friction once read "Great" on wet rock.

Evidence against: the Met Office game (n=8,220) found numbers beat low/medium/high at equal information. A word band adds a second, coarser claim on top of the number, and any wrong "Good" is a stronger miss than a wrong 85. Thursday shows the risk: "Good" on a day it rains from 2 PM.

Verdict sentence

  • One generated sentence under the score, replacing the figure line: now, the rest of today, the next rain and when the rock is dry again. Dark Sky's "light rain starting in 4 minutes" is the feature users remember.
  • Built from the same fields as variant A's Rain rows, by a template, never by an AI summary.

Breaks copy rule 3 (a reading is a label and a value, never a sentence) and edges rule 1: "the rock should be dry again" is a claim about the rock in prose. "Dry and cool now, good until 6 PM" reads as advice.

Compare: Overview variant A carries every fact in this sentence as three rows. The sentence is faster to read; the rows are easier to check one by one.

Marked as an estimate

  • An "Estimate" tag on the score and a track record line: how many of your checks here matched.
  • The top held back: a day over 80 prints "80+" until a check within three days backs it, the way Surfline lets only an observing forecaster award "Good". Today keeps 92 because Saturday's check matched.

Breaks the score rules: the score is labelled "Score at", never qualified, and nothing reaches past score. "80+" also hides a number the model has, which runs against "withhold, never inflate" in the other direction: it withholds a real figure. Safety and product call.

Data: your own checks exist. "Matched" needs a rule for what counts as a match (a dry report at 60 or more?), which is #143's open question.

Alerts

  • A push when a crag crosses your threshold on the days and hours you set, a few days ahead. CragReport and Acme offer this.
  • The settings sit on the crag's Overview; they could live on Profile instead.

Parked: there is no notification channel (owner's parked decision, architecture.md). A PWA can push on Android and on iOS 16.4+ once installed to the home screen; it needs a push service, stored subscriptions and a cron to evaluate thresholds.

Risk: notification spam is 12% of weather app complaints. A threshold alert that fires on a score the forecast later drops would be the loudest miss the app could make.

The rules behind section 3

  • A word band beside the score (Good, Caution, Poor), which every climbing app found uses. Copy rule 1: no word is mapped from the score. The Met Office game also found numbers beat low/medium/high at equal information, so the rule and the evidence agree here.
  • A verdict sentence ("Dry until 2 PM, showers to 6 PM"). Copy rule 3: a reading is a label and a value. Variant A carries the same facts as rows; a sentence would need the rule changed.
  • Marking the score unvalidated, or holding back the top of the scale until a check backs it. A product and safety call; variant B's Forecast checks is the softer version.
  • "Crag becomes climbable" alerts. Parked: there is no notification channel.
